What is a Stock?

5 min Charts

When a company wants to raise money to grow, it can sell small pieces of ownership to the public. Each piece is called a share. The collection of all shares is called stock. When you buy one share of Apple (AAPL), you literally own a tiny fraction of Apple Inc.

Definition
Stock

A type of security that represents fractional ownership of a corporation. Owning stock entitles you to a claim on the company's assets and earnings proportional to your share count.

Key Terms You Need

Share

One unit of ownership in a company. If a company has 1,000 shares and you own 10, you own 1% of it.

Market Cap

Total value of all shares. Formula: Share Price × Shares Outstanding. Apple's market cap is over $2 trillion.

Shares Outstanding

Total number of shares a company has issued to all investors, including insiders and public investors.

Dividend

A cash payment a company makes to shareholders, usually quarterly. Not all companies pay dividends.

Ticker Symbol

A short code used to identify a stock on an exchange. AAPL = Apple, TSLA = Tesla, NVDA = NVIDIA.

IPO

Initial Public Offering — the first time a company sells shares to the public. This is how a company "goes public."

Formula
Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding
Example: Apple stock at $189 × 15.4 billion shares = ~$2.9 trillion market cap. This is why Apple is called a "mega-cap" company.

Why do stock prices move?

Prices move because of supply and demand. If more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price rises. If more people want to sell, it falls. What drives those decisions? Earnings reports, economic data, news, and market sentiment.

Supply vs. Demand — how buying pressure pushes prices up

Each upward wave represents a period where buyers outnumbered sellers. Downward waves show selling pressure exceeding demand.

Real-world example

Imagine a pizza shop worth $1,000,000 that divides itself into 100,000 shares. Each share is worth $10. If you buy 100 shares for $1,000, you own 0.1% of the business. If the shop becomes worth $2,000,000, your 100 shares are now worth $2,000 — a 100% gain. This is the same mechanic behind every stock trade.

Reading a Stock Chart

8 min Charts + patterns 3 quizzes

A stock chart is a visual record of a stock's price over time. Understanding how to read one is the single most important skill in trading. The most widely used format is the candlestick chart.

Anatomy of a Candlestick

Each candle represents one time period (1 day, 1 hour, etc.) and contains four key data points:

Open

The price at the start of the time period. This is one edge of the candle body.

Close

The price at the end of the period. This is the other edge of the body. If Close > Open, it's a green (bullish) candle.

High

The highest price traded during the period. Shown as the top wick (also called shadow).

Low

The lowest price traded during the period. Shown as the bottom wick.

Bullish
Bearish
Top wick: Highest price traded in the period
Green body: Price closed higher than open — bullish candle
Red body: Price closed lower than open — bearish candle
Bottom wick: Lowest price traded in the period
30-Day Price Chart with Moving Average

Green bars = bullish days, red bars = bearish days. The blue line is a 10-day moving average, smoothing out short-term noise to show the trend direction.

Essential Chart Concepts

Formula — Simple Moving Average (SMA)
SMA(N) = (P₁ + P₂ + ... + Pₙ) ÷ N
Example: The 20-day SMA = sum of the last 20 closing prices divided by 20. It removes day-to-day noise and shows the average price trend over that window.
Pattern example — Support bounce

A stock trades between $45 and $55 for several weeks. Every time it drops to $45, buyers come in and push it back up. That $45 level is strong support. A trader might buy near $45 with a stop-loss at $43, targeting $52 — a risk of $2 to make $7 (1:3.5 risk/reward).

Certain candlestick shapes repeat across markets and carry predictive meaning. These are the ones every trader recognizes:

Important nuance
Context matters more than the candle shape.

A hammer at the bottom of a downtrend is a strong reversal signal. The same hammer in the middle of an uptrend means very little. Always read candles in the context of the surrounding trend, support/resistance, and volume.

Reading Volume

Volume is the number of shares traded in a period. It's the conviction meter behind every price move. A price move with high volume is real. The same move with low volume may be a fake-out.

High Volume Breakout

Price breaks above resistance with 2× or more the average volume. This is the most reliable breakout signal — institutions are participating.

Low Volume Rally

Price rises but volume is weak. This suggests retail-driven buying without institutional confirmation. Treat with suspicion.

Volume Climax

Extremely high volume after a prolonged move often signals exhaustion — the last buyers have bought. Can mark a top or bottom.

Declining Volume in Trend

An uptrend with progressively falling volume suggests the move is losing steam. Watch for a trend reversal.

Risk & Reward

6 min Formulas

Every trade has a risk-to-reward ratio. This is the most important concept for staying in the game long term. Most new traders focus on whether they're right. Professional traders focus on how much they make when right vs. how much they lose when wrong.

Definition
Risk-to-Reward Ratio (R:R)

The ratio of potential loss (risk) to potential gain (reward) on a trade. A 1:3 ratio means you risk $1 to potentially make $3.

Formula
R:R = (Entry Price − Stop Loss) : (Target Price − Entry Price)
Example: Buy at $50, stop-loss at $47, target $59. Risk = $3. Reward = $9. R:R = 1:3.

The Math
Why 1:3 is so powerful

With a 1:3 ratio, you only need to be right 25% of the time to break even. At 50% accuracy, you're very profitable. Most beginners trade 1:1 or worse — which requires winning over 60% of trades just to not lose money.

Win rate needed to break even at each R:R ratio

The better your risk/reward ratio, the less often you need to be right. Professional traders optimize R:R before worrying about win rate.

Position Sizing

Position sizing determines how many shares to buy so you never risk too much of your account on one trade.

Formula — Position Size
Shares = (Account × Risk %) ÷ (Entry Price − Stop Price)
Example: $100,000 account, 1% risk, entry at $50, stop at $47. Dollar risk = $1,000. Distance to stop = $3. Shares = 1,000 ÷ 3 = 333 shares.

Worked example

You have $100,000 in your Hedgex account. You want to buy AAPL at $189, with a stop at $185, targeting $201. Risk per share = $4. Reward per share = $12. R:R = 1:3. Max 1% risk = $1,000 ÷ $4 = 250 shares. Total position = $47,250 — less than 50% of your account.

Quiz 1: A trader has a $50,000 account and wants to risk 2% per trade. Their entry is $80, stop-loss is $75. How many shares can they buy?

Not quite. Step 1: Dollar risk = $50,000 × 2% = $1,000. Step 2: Stop distance = $80 − $75 = $5. Step 3: Shares = $1,000 ÷ $5 = 200 shares.
Quiz 2: With a 1:4 risk-to-reward ratio, what is the minimum win rate needed to be profitable over many trades?
A50% — you always need to be right more than wrong
B33% — you need to win 1 in 3 trades
C20% — at 1:4, winning 1 in 5 trades breaks even

Correct! Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward ratio) = 1 ÷ 5 = 20%. At 1:4, you win $4 for every $1 risked, so you only need 1 win per 5 trades to break even. This is the mathematical power of a high R:R ratio.
Not quite. Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward ratio). At 1:4 that's 1 ÷ 5 = 20%. You can be wrong 80% of the time and still break even — as long as your winners are 4× your losers.

Order Types

5 min Definitions

Placing the wrong type of order can cost you money even when your trade idea is right. Here are the four order types you need to know — and when Hedgex accepts them.

Important
Hedgex uses market orders during trading hours.

The Flask backend executes trades at the live Yahoo Finance price at the time you submit. Trading is only available Monday–Friday, 8:30 AM – 3:00 PM CST.

Order TypeHow it worksBest ForRisk

Slippage

The difference between the expected fill price and the actual fill price. More common in fast markets and with market orders on low-volume stocks.

Fill

When your order is matched with a counterparty and executed. "Getting filled" means your trade went through.

Bid/Ask Spread

The difference between the highest price a buyer will pay (bid) and the lowest price a seller will accept (ask). Wider spread = higher trading cost.

FIFO

First In, First Out — Hedgex sells your oldest shares first when you close a position. This is the standard accounting method.

Quiz: You want to buy TSLA at exactly $245.00 but the current price is $248. Which order type is correct?
AMarket order — fills immediately at the best available price
BLimit order at $245 — only fills if price drops to your level
CStop-loss at $245 — triggers when price falls to $245
DStop-limit at $248 — protects against further upside
Correct! A buy limit order at $245 sits in the order book waiting. If TSLA drops to $245, your order fills. If it never reaches $245, you don't buy — which is fine. You controlled your entry price instead of chasing.
Not quite. A limit buy order at $245 will only execute if the price drops to $245 or below. A market order would fill immediately at $248 — paying more than you wanted. A stop-loss at $245 would trigger a sell, not a buy.

Technical Indicators

12 min Charts + formulas

Technical indicators are mathematical calculations applied to price and volume data. They help you identify trends, momentum, and potential entry/exit points. The key is not to use all of them — it's to understand a few deeply.

RSI — Relative Strength Index

Definition
RSI (Relative Strength Index)

A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes on a scale from 0 to 100. Developed by J. Welles Wilder in 1978.

Formula
RSI = 100 – [100 ÷ (1 + RS)] where RS = Avg Gain ÷ Avg Loss over N periods
Default is 14 periods. RS is the ratio of the average gain days to average loss days over 14 sessions. An RSI of 70+ means buyers have been dominant — potentially overbought. Below 30 means sellers have dominated — potentially oversold.
RSI — overbought and oversold zones

Red zone (above 70) = potential sell signal. Green zone (below 30) = potential buy signal. RSI is most useful in range-bound, non-trending markets.

MACD — Moving Average Convergence/Divergence

Definition
MACD

A trend-following momentum indicator showing the relationship between two exponential moving averages. It shows when bullish or bearish momentum is building.

Formula
MACD Line = EMA(12) – EMA(26) | Signal Line = EMA(9) of MACD | Histogram = MACD – Signal
EMA = Exponential Moving Average (weights recent prices more). When MACD crosses above the Signal Line = bullish signal. When it crosses below = bearish signal. The Histogram shows the gap growing or shrinking.

Bollinger Bands

Formula
Upper Band = SMA(20) + 2σ | Lower Band = SMA(20) – 2σ
σ (sigma) = standard deviation of the last 20 closing prices. The bands expand during high volatility and contract when the market is calm. Price touching the upper band isn't automatically a sell signal — in a strong uptrend, price can "ride" the band.

Quick Reference — All Three Indicators

RSI
Momentum · 0–100

Buy signal: RSI crosses above 30 from below. Sell signal: RSI crosses below 70 from above.

MACD
Trend Momentum · Signal Cross

Buy: MACD crosses above Signal Line. Sell: MACD crosses below Signal Line. Watch the histogram.

Bollinger Bands
Volatility · 2σ envelope

Squeeze (bands narrowing) = big move coming. Price > upper band = high volatility, not necessarily sell.

Formula — PEG Ratio (Peter Lynch's favorite)
PEG = P/E Ratio ÷ Annual EPS Growth Rate (%)
Example: A company with a P/E of 20 and 20% annual EPS growth has a PEG of 1.0 — fairly valued. A company with P/E of 40 and 10% growth has a PEG of 4.0 — expensive relative to its growth.
Revenue growth — comparing fast vs. slow growing companies

High revenue growth usually justifies a higher P/E. When growth slows, the market often reprices the stock downward — even if it's still profitable.

Key principle
Always compare within the same sector.

A P/E of 30 is cheap for a high-growth software company but expensive for a utility or bank. Use sector medians as your benchmark, not the overall S&P 500 average of ~20x.

Full fundamental analysis example — NVDA

NVIDIA (NVDA) has a P/E of ~71x — much higher than the S&P 500 average. But its EPS grew 152% in 2024. PEG = 71 ÷ 152 = 0.47 — which actually suggests it may be undervalued relative to its growth rate. Revenue growing at 122% YoY, gross margins of 78%, and near-zero debt. A fundamental analyst might conclude the premium valuation is justified by AI chip demand — but carries execution risk if growth slows.

Quiz: A company has a P/E of 45 and annual EPS growth of 45%. Its PEG ratio is:

Correct! PEG = P/E ÷ EPS growth rate = 45 ÷ 45 = 1.0. A PEG of 1.0 is generally considered fairly valued. Below 1.0 = potentially undervalued relative to growth; above 2.0 = expensive relative to growth.
Not quite. PEG = P/E ÷ Annual EPS Growth Rate (%). So 45 ÷ 45 = 1.0. Peter Lynch popularized PEG — he considered anything below 1.0 potentially cheap and above 2.0 potentially expensive.

News & Market Sentiment

9 min Real examples

Market prices reflect not just a company's financials — they also reflect how investors feel about the future. Understanding how to read news and gauge market sentiment is one of the most powerful (and underrated) skills in investing.

Definition
Market Sentiment

The overall attitude of investors toward a particular stock or the broader market. Sentiment is bullish when investors are optimistic and expect prices to rise, and bearish when they expect prices to fall.

Types of Market-Moving News

📈
Earnings Reports
Quarterly results — revenue, EPS, and forward guidance. The most predictable and high-impact event.
📋
Analyst Ratings
Upgrades (Buy), downgrades (Sell), price target changes. Can move stocks 3–8% on a single note.
🏢
Fed Announcements
Interest rate decisions move the entire market. Rate hikes are bearish for growth stocks; cuts are bullish.
📰
Macro Economic Data
CPI (inflation), jobs reports, GDP — these move market sentiment before individual stocks.
⚠
Geopolitical Events
Wars, trade disputes, elections. Create fear and uncertainty — usually short-term but extreme impact.
🛠
Product Launches
New products, partnerships, or patents. Apple's iPhone announcements historically move AAPL ±3%.

How to Read an Earnings Report

Earnings are released every quarter. The market reacts to whether results beat or miss analyst expectations — not to whether results are good or bad in absolute terms.

MetricWhat "beat" meansWhat "miss" meansTypical reaction
RevenueActual revenue > consensus estimateActual revenue < consensus estimateBeat = +2–5%, Miss = -3–8%
EPSActual earnings per share > estimateActual EPS < estimateEPS beats often matter more than revenue
GuidanceNext quarter outlook above expectationsNext quarter outlook below expectationsGuidance often moves stock more than results
Gross MarginMargins expanding quarter-over-quarterMargins compressingMargin expansion = efficiency improvement signal
Key concept
"Buy the rumor, sell the news"

Stocks often rise before a positive announcement as investors anticipate good news. Once the news is actually released — even if good — the stock can sell off because buyers have already positioned and are now taking profit. This is called a sell-the-news event. NVDA regularly beat earnings and dropped the same day.

How to calculate earnings surprise %
Surprise % = [(Actual EPS − Estimated EPS) ÷ |Estimated EPS|] × 100
Example: NVDA estimated EPS $5.60, actual EPS $6.12. Surprise = [(6.12 − 5.60) ÷ 5.60] × 100 = +9.3% positive surprise. A surprise above 5% typically produces a strong gap-up at open.
How to research sentiment before taking a trade

1. Check the earnings calendar — is earnings in the next 2 weeks? Avoid holding through earnings unless you have a specific thesis. 2. Look at analyst consensus on Yahoo Finance under "Analysis" — how many Buy/Hold/Sell ratings? 3. Check the VIX — is the market fearful or complacent? 4. Search recent news headlines for the ticker — any SEC filings, lawsuits, or partnerships you're not aware of? 5. Look at short interest — a heavily shorted stock can spike violently on good news (short squeeze).

Quiz: A company reports earnings that beat analyst EPS estimates by 12%, but their guidance for next quarter comes in below expectations. What typically happens to the stock?

Correct! The market is forward-looking. Strong past earnings plus weak guidance = "the best is behind us" narrative. Stocks frequently sell off after earnings beats when guidance disappoints. This is why guidance is often more important than the actual quarterly results.

Weak guidance signals the company expects conditions to worsen — and the stock typically falls even on a strong beat. This is one of the most counterintuitive things about earnings season.

Start with swing trading.

Day trading requires near-instant decisions and statistically produces the worst results for new traders. Practice swing trading first — hold positions for 2–10 days, focus on chart patterns and R:R ratios. The Hedgex simulator is designed for this style.

A complete swing trade example

You see MSFT forming a cup-and-handle pattern on a daily chart, with RSI at 52 (neutral) and MACD crossing up. Entry: $410 on a volume breakout. Stop: $404 (just below the handle low). Target: $426 (measured move based on cup depth). Risk = $6, Reward = $16 → R:R = 1:2.7. Position size: 1% account risk = $1,000 ÷ $6 = 166 shares.

Quiz: A day trader and a swing trader both buy the same stock at $100. The day trader sells at $102 (+2%) the same day. The swing trader holds 8 days and sells at $112 (+12%). Who made the "better" trade, and why might both be valid?
AThe swing trader — a 12% gain is always better than 2%
BThe day trader — less time in the market = less risk
CBoth valid — each executed their strategy correctly; the right choice depends on your time, risk tolerance, and system
DNeither — you should never sell before a 20% gain
Correct! Both trades are valid if each trader followed their pre-defined strategy. The day trader took a clean 2% with zero overnight risk. The swing trader accepted 8 days of price risk for a 12% gain. Neither is objectively better — what matters is consistency and discipline within your own system.
Not quite. Both are valid depending on the trader's strategy. The key insight: a "good trade" isn't measured by profit alone — it's measured by whether you followed your process. A 2% gain from a flawless day trade is just as valid as a 12% swing trade gain.

Trading Psychology

9 min Case studies 2 quizzes

Most traders fail not because of bad strategy — but because of psychological mistakes. The market is designed to exploit human instincts. Your instincts were built for survival, not for navigating volatile asset prices.

The Four Biggest Biases

Emotional
Fear & Panic Selling

Normal pullbacks trigger premature exits from perfectly good trades. Fear makes you sell at exactly the wrong moment.

Example: Selling NVDA at a 4% pullback only to watch it recover 18% over the next two weeks.

Emotional
Greed & Overconfidence

Holding past your target, over-sizing during winning streaks, or adding to losing positions because you're "sure" it'll recover.

Example: Your $300 target gets hit but you hold for $350 because it "feels" like it'll keep going. It doesn't.

Cognitive
Confirmation Bias

Only consuming information that supports your existing trade idea. Ignoring red flags that would force you to rethink.

Example: Only reading bullish analyst reports on a stock you already own, ignoring negative earnings revisions.

Cognitive
Loss Aversion

Research shows losses feel ~2× more painful than equivalent gains. This leads to holding losers too long and cutting winners too early.

Example: Refusing to sell a -35% position because "selling makes the loss real." Meanwhile it falls to -60%.

Emotional cycle of a new trader

Every new trader follows a predictable emotional arc. The goal of practice trading is to experience and survive this cycle with virtual capital first.

The Fix
Write your trade plan before entering any position.

Before entering a trade, write down: (1) entry price, (2) stop-loss, (3) target, (4) R:R ratio, (5) reason for the trade. When emotions hit during the trade, refer to the plan — not your feelings. If the plan still makes sense, stay in. If conditions changed, exit.

How to Build Mental Discipline

Trade Journal

Write down every trade: entry, exit, reasoning, and how you felt. Review weekly. Patterns in your mistakes become obvious very quickly.

Pre-Trade Checklist

Before entering: Does this match my strategy? Is the R:R at least 1:2? Am I trading out of boredom or FOMO? If any answer is wrong, skip the trade.

Set Hard Rules

Max loss per day. Max positions at once. No trading the first 30 minutes of market open. Rules remove in-the-moment decisions that emotions corrupt.

Pause After Losses

"Revenge trading" — immediately trying to win back a loss — is one of the most common and destructive patterns. After a loss, stop for the day.

The FOMO Trap

FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) causes traders to chase stocks that have already moved significantly. You see NVDA up 8% and jump in near the top — right as early buyers are selling their profits into your buy order. This is called being "the exit liquidity."

FOMO vs. Discipline — a real pattern

NVDA reports earnings and gaps up 12% at open. The disciplined trader had a plan: "I'll only buy NVDA if it pulls back to its prior support at $820 with strong volume." The FOMO trader buys the gap-up at $870. By 2pm, NVDA has "sold the news" back to $840. The disciplined trader either waits for the setup or misses the trade entirely — which is fine. The FOMO trader is down $30/share and now makes emotional decisions to hold or cut. Missing a trade costs nothing. Chasing one can cost a lot.

The Dunning-Kruger Curve in Trading

Most new traders go through a predictable confidence cycle:

PhaseWhat happensCommon mistakeExit strategy
1. ExcitementFirst wins, overconfidence, "this is easy"Over-sizing positions, ignoring riskKeep position sizes small regardless of wins
2. Reality CheckFirst losing streak, losses larger than expectedRevenge trading, abandoning the planStick strictly to stop-losses, review journal
3. FrustrationConvinced the market is rigged against youQuitting before learning the real lessonsRead trade journal, find pattern in mistakes
4. AcceptanceUnderstanding that losses are part of the processStill letting emotions creep into decisionsPre-trade checklist every single time
5. ConsistencySystematic approach, R:R driven, emotion-reducedComplacency, skipping rules after a hot streakNever stop following the process
Quiz 1: A trader has a winning streak of 5 profitable trades. On trade 6, they size up to 3× their normal position "because they're on a roll." This is an example of which bias?
ALoss Aversion — they fear missing a gain
BConfirmation Bias — they're seeking proof they're right
COverconfidence / Greed — recent wins inflated their risk tolerance
DRecency Bias — they think recent patterns will continue forever
Correct! Overconfidence after a win streak is one of the most dangerous patterns. Each trade should be sized the same regardless of recent performance — the market doesn't know or care about your streak.
Not quite. This is primarily overconfidence / greed — the trader believes they're "hot" and increases risk beyond their normal plan. Recency bias plays a supporting role, but greed is the driving force here.
Quiz 2: A trader buys a stock at $50. It drops to $35. Instead of selling at their $45 stop-loss (which they ignored), they say "I'll hold until it comes back to $50." This is primarily:
ASmart long-term investing — patience always pays off
BConfirmation Bias — they're reading bullish articles to justify holding
CLoss Aversion — selling makes the loss "real," so they avoid it even at greater cost
DCorrect risk management — you should never sell at a loss
Correct! Loss Aversion in action. By ignoring the $45 stop-loss, the trader let a $500 loss ($50→$45 × 10 shares) become a $1,500 loss ($50→$35). The psychological resistance to "making it real" by selling often causes far larger losses.
Not quite. This is Loss Aversion — the pain of realizing a loss feels worse than watching it grow unrealized. The correct action was to honor the $45 stop-loss and cut the position. Stop-losses exist precisely for this scenario.
